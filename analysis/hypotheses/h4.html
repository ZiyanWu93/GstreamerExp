SCReAM vs GCC across three latency deadlines

main finding

On a fluctuating network, GCC delivers more frames to the viewer than SCReAM in every condition tested. Neither the deadline nor retransmission changes the ranking.

  • The ranking is set by how the encoder is paced through the bottleneck. During the 300 kbps phase, GCC throttles the encoder more aggressively than SCReAM does. GCC's smaller frames fit through the bottleneck and decode at the viewer; SCReAM's larger frames overflow the pipe, lose packets, and fail to decode.
  • The latency deadline doesn't move this gap meaningfully. On the constant-network version of this test, a 50 ms deadline reversed the SCReAM-vs-GCC ranking. On a fluctuating network, the gap is set during the bottleneck phase regardless of deadline — adding a deadline just clips a few additional frames from each arm without changing who's ahead.
  • Retransmission also doesn't move the gap meaningfully. Whether retransmission is on or off, the SCReAM-vs-GCC gap is ~34–41 frames in GCC's favor. The bottleneck's dominant effect is on encoder pacing, not on packet recovery.

Two video congestion-control algorithms — SCReAM and GCC — disagree on how fast a sender should push bits into a network with packet loss. Retransmission resends lost packets, which lifts frame count on the wire but adds latency. A latency budget drops frames that arrive past a deadline, on the operational principle that a stale frame is useless to a teleoperator. This page asks: which CC algorithm delivers more on-time frames, and how does that change with the deadline?

Setup. Capacity-step network: 5 Mbps clean for 7 seconds, drops to 300 kbps with 2% packet loss for 6 seconds, recovers to 5 Mbps for 7 seconds. The loss happens during the bottleneck phase, where retransmission has the most work to do but the least bandwidth to do it on. Three deadlines tested (0 ms = no enforcement, 50 ms, 100 ms) crossed with retransmission on/off and SCReAM/GCC, 5 reps per cell. Numbers below use per-rep median to avoid single-run outliers dominating means.

The comparison, at a glance

How to read the plot above.
  • X-axis: time since run start, in seconds.
  • Y-axis: bytes per second arriving at the viewer.
  • Yellow shaded window: the 6-second 300 kbps bottleneck phase (capacity drops from 5 Mbps).
  • Four lines: SCReAM (red) and GCC (blue), each in two flavours: solid = no retransmission, no deadline (the cleanest baseline); dashed = retransmission on, 50 ms deadline (the tightest tested condition).
  • Box at top right: total median frames delivered per 20-second run for each condition.

What to look at. If the two red lines overlap and the two blue lines overlap, retransmission and deadline don't change what arrives. The vertical separation between red and blue is the SCReAM-vs-GCC gap, and it's set during the yellow bottleneck window — that's where SCReAM's larger frames overflow the 300 kbps pipe and fail to decode.

Subclaim 1 · The 34–41-frame GCC advantage is set during the bottleneck phase

All twelve cells of this experiment share the same 6-second bottleneck where capacity drops to 300 kbps with 2% loss. The SCReAM-vs-GCC gap is essentially identical across cells, so it isn't being created by the deadline or by retransmission — it's created during the bottleneck phase by the encoder pacing each algorithm chooses. SCReAM keeps the encoder at a higher target rate during the squeeze; GCC throttles harder. SCReAM's larger frames overflow the 300 kbps pipe and fail to decode at the viewer; GCC's smaller frames fit through and arrive intact.

Subclaim 2 · The deadline doesn't change the ranking on this network

On the constant-network version of this test, a 50 ms deadline reversed the SCReAM-vs-GCC ranking. On the fluctuating network, the same deadline cuts a few frames from both algorithms but doesn't meaningfully change the gap. The fluctuating-network gap is dominated by frames lost to bottleneck overflow (which the deadline can't fix), not by frames arriving slightly late (which the deadline targets).

Subclaim 3 · Retransmission also doesn't change the ranking

Turning retransmission on or off shifts the gap by at most 3 frames. Retransmission rescues some packets that would otherwise drop, but the dominant frame-count loss on this network comes from encoder over-production during the bottleneck — frames the bottleneck couldn't carry in the first place, so retransmission has nothing to rescue.

Glossary

SCReAM, GCC
Two congestion-control algorithms for real-time video. They decide how fast the sender should push bits given network conditions; they sense the network differently.
Retransmission (NACK)
The receiver tells the sender which packets were lost; the sender resends them. Implemented in real-time video as the NACK protocol. Adds at least one round-trip of latency per retransmitted packet, plus a longer wait at the receiver to give resends time to arrive.
Latency budget / deadline
The maximum age (since capture) at which a frame is still operationally useful. Frames older than the budget at the viewer sink are dropped. budget=0 means no enforcement.
Delivered (within budget)
Frames that arrived at the viewer within the deadline. With no deadline, every received frame counts.
p99 lateness
The lateness value such that 99% of frames in a run were less late. Captures the "long tail" of slow frames. Higher = a worse late tail.
Median
The middle value across runs. Used here instead of the mean to avoid a single bad run dominating the comparison.
